Tea Prior Notice: FDA Required Notification For Every Shipment

FDA prior notice tea import 21 CFR 1.279 US customs

If you are sending tea into the United States, there is one step that catches out more newcomers than any other: FDA prior notice. It is not paperwork you can tidy up after the container lands. It must be filed and confirmed before the shipment arrives, and the clock is measured in hours.

This guide walks through what prior notice is, who must file it, the exact deadlines in the rule, and the consequences of getting it wrong. Everything here comes from 21 CFR Part 1, Subpart I — the governing text — and the official links are at the end.

What prior notice actually is

Prior notice is an advance notification to the US Food and Drug Administration that food is being imported or offered for import. Tea is a food, so it falls squarely within the rule. Under § 1.277(a), the requirement applies to all food for humans and animals imported or offered for import — including gifts, samples, transshipments, and goods going into foreign trade zones.

That broad wording is important. You cannot assume your shipment is exempt just because it is small, free, or not a commercial sale. A sample sent to a customer, a gift, or a shipment that will later move on to another country is still within scope.

There are exemptions in § 1.277(b), but they are narrow. They include personal food carried with a traveller, non-commercial personal gifts, food imported and then exported without leaving the port, meat, poultry and egg products under USDA exclusive jurisdiction, and diplomatic mail. A normal commercial tea shipment does not fit any of these.

The deadlines: the part everyone needs to memorise

This is the heart of the rule. Section 1.279 sets out how far in advance prior notice must be submitted and confirmed by FDA for review. Note both verbs — sending the notice is not enough; FDA must have confirmed it for review.

The minimum advance times, measured before arrival, are:

  • Road arrival: no less than 2 hours before arrival.
  • Rail or air arrival: no less than 4 hours before arrival.
  • Water arrival by vessel: no less than 8 hours before arrival.

For international mail, § 1.279(c) sets a different trigger: prior notice is required before the article is sent to the United States. There is no arrival window to rely on there, so the timing has to be built into your dispatch process.

There is also an earliest window — you cannot file too far in advance. Section 1.279(b) says prior notice may not be submitted more than:

  • 30 calendar days before anticipated arrival when filed through ABI/ACE/ITDS, or
  • 15 calendar days before anticipated arrival when filed through FDA PNSI.

So there is a usable filing window: not later than the minimum hours before arrival, and not earlier than the day limits above. A practical approach is to prepare the data early and file within that window, then confirm FDA's review rather than assuming the submission went through.

Two cautions. First, these are the deadlines in the current text of § 1.279 — always check the current eCFR before a shipment, because rules and systems can change. Second, "hours before arrival" is not the same as "hours before departure." Build in a buffer for time zones, carrier schedules and the fact that someone has to confirm the review.

How to file, and in what language

Section 1.280 says prior notice must be submitted electronically to FDA. The two practical routes are:

  • Through the customs entry system — ABI/ACE/ITDS — which is typical for commercial entries handled by a broker; or
  • Through the FDA Prior Notice System Interface, known as PNSI, at https://www.access.fda.gov/.

The information must be provided in English. There is one narrow allowance: proper names and street names may appear in a foreign language, but they must be in the Latin or Roman alphabet. If your supplier's details are in Chinese characters, they need to be romanised for the filing. This is a frequent source of small errors, so gather the English or romanised details well before you file.

What the notice must contain

Section 1.281 lists the information required. For a tea importer, the useful way to read the list is as a data checklist. You will need:

  • The name, address, phone number and email of the person submitting the notice and, if different, the transmitter.
  • The entry type and the FDA product code for the tea.
  • The common or usual name of the article — for example, "green tea," "black tea" or "pu-erh tea."
  • The quantity.
  • Lot or code numbers, where available.
  • Manufacturer or grower information.
  • The FDA country of production, and the country from which the article is shipped.
  • The port of arrival, and the anticipated date and time of arrival.
  • The importer, owner and ultimate consignee.
  • The mode of transport, carrier code, and shipment information such as the bill of lading, airway bill or vessel name.
  • Whether the food has been previously refused admission by any country.

That last item surprises people. If a previous shipment was refused somewhere, it is not optional information. The honest answer is required, and a "no" that later proves wrong turns a paperwork issue into a credibility problem.

Notice how much of this is ordinary commercial data. A supplier who cannot give you a grower, a lot number or a clear product name is making your filing harder. This is one reason importers screen their suppliers before goods ship, not after.

What goes wrong, and what it costs

Section 1.283 is blunt. If you submit no prior notice, an inaccurate prior notice, or a late prior notice, the food is subject to refusal of admission under section 801(m)(1) of the FD&C Act. In plain terms, it can be refused entry.

The rule does provide routes back:

  • You may re-submit within 10 calendar days of the notice of refusal.
  • You may request FDA review within 5 calendar days.
  • For international mail, CBP may hold the article for up to 72 hours.

Those are tight windows. A refused shipment sitting at a port is not just an abstract legal problem — it means storage costs, delayed delivery and an unhappy customer.

One more distinction that trips people up. Section 1.285 deals with food from unregistered facilities. If the food is from a facility that is not registered with FDA, it can be held under section 801(l), and a valid registration number must be submitted within 30 calendar days. That is a separate issue from prior notice timing. A perfectly filed prior notice does not cure an unregistered facility, and a registered facility does not excuse a late notice. Both obligations stand on their own.

Prior notice is not the whole import picture

It helps to keep prior notice in context. It is an advance notification step. It sits alongside other US requirements, such as the Foreign Supplier Verification Program under 21 CFR Part 1 Subpart L, and facility registration under the FD&C Act. Clearing one does not clear the others. If you are new to this, treat prior notice as the first clock to master, then work through the rest of the import file systematically.

Bottom line

Prior notice is required for essentially every commercial tea shipment entering the United States, with only narrow exemptions. Under 21 CFR 1.279, it must be submitted and confirmed by FDA at least 2 hours before road arrival, 4 hours before rail or air arrival, and 8 hours before water arrival; international mail needs notice before it is sent. Do not file earlier than 30 calendar days via ABI/ACE/ITDS or 15 calendar days via PNSI. File electronically, in English with romanised names, gather the § 1.281 data from your supplier in advance, and remember that no, inaccurate or late notice can lead to refusal of admission. Check the current eCFR text before each shipment.
