Why Tea Fails Customs Check In The United States

US customs FDA prior notice FSVP tea import import alert

When a container of tea is stopped at a US port, the cause is often not what importers expect. It is rarely a dramatic lab failure. More often it is a missed deadline, a missing programme, or a name that appears on the wrong list. The United States has a paperwork-heavy import system, and tea is ordinary food in the eyes of the law — which means the ordinary food rules apply in full.

This article sets out the main reasons a tea shipment gets refused or held, and what you can do to avoid them.

Reason 1: Prior Notice was late, wrong or missing

The first hurdle is Prior Notice, governed by 21 CFR Part 1 Subpart I. It applies to all food for humans and animals imported or offered for import. That includes gifts, samples, transshipments and goods going into foreign trade zones. It is broad.

Prior Notice must be submitted electronically to the FDA. The usual routes are through ABI/ACE/ITDS or the FDA's own Prior Notice System Interface (PNSI). The information is submitted in English; names and street names may be in a foreign language, but they must be in the Latin or Roman alphabet.

The timing rules are strict, and this is where many shipments come unstuck. Under § 1.279, Prior Notice must be submitted and confirmed by the FDA for review no less than:

  • 2 hours before arrival for road transport;
  • 4 hours before arrival for rail or air;
  • 8 hours before arrival for water transport (vessel).

For international mail, Prior Notice must be given before the article is sent to the United States. There is also an earliest window: no more than 30 calendar days before anticipated arrival when using ABI/ACE/ITDS, and no more than 15 calendar days when using FDA PNSI.

A common mistake is to treat these as suggestions, or to send the notice at the port instead of before arrival. Another is to get a detail wrong. The required content under § 1.281 includes submitter and transmitter information, entry type, FDA product code, the common or usual name, quantity, lot or code numbers, manufacturer or grower information, FDA country of production, shipper, country of shipment, port of arrival with date and time, importer, owner, ultimate consignee, mode of transport, carrier code and shipment details such as the bill of lading. You must also say whether the food was previously refused by any country.

Getting one of these fields wrong counts as inaccurate Prior Notice.

What happens when Prior Notice fails

The consequences are set out in § 1.283. No Prior Notice, inaccurate Prior Notice or late Prior Notice leads to refusal of admission under section 801(m)(1) of the FD&C Act. This is a refusal at the border, not a fine you can quietly pay later.

There are limited recovery steps. You may re-submit within 10 calendar days of the refusal notice, or request FDA review within 5 calendar days. For international mail, CBP may hold the article for up to 72 hours. Those windows are short, which is why getting it right the first time matters.

Reason 2: The facility is not registered with FDA

A separate issue trips up new importers: FDA facility registration. Food facilities, both in the United States and abroad, that manufacture, process, pack or hold food for US consumption must register with the FDA under section 415 of the FD&C Act.

Prior Notice alone is not enough. If the food comes from an unregistered facility, § 1.285 allows it to be held under section 801(l) of the Act. To release it, a valid registration number must be submitted within 30 calendar days.

This is different from the Prior Notice timing problem. You can file perfect Prior Notice and still have a shipment held because the facility producing the tea is not registered.

Reason 3: No proper FSVP in place

The Foreign Supplier Verification Programs rule, 21 CFR Part 1 Subpart L, is based on section 805 of the FD&C Act. It puts a real duty on the US importer — defined in § 1.500 as the US owner or consignee at the time of entry, or, if there is none, the US agent or representative of the foreign owner or consignee with signed consent.

Under § 1.502(a), the importer must develop, maintain and follow an FSVP that gives adequate assurance that the foreign supplier uses processes providing at least the same level of public health protection as US requirements, and complies with the relevant provisions of the Act. This programme must be developed and performed by a qualified individual with the education, training or experience to do it, and any onsite audits must be done by a qualified auditor.

A compliant FSVP is not a single form. It includes a written hazard analysis under § 1.504, covering known or reasonably foreseeable biological, chemical and physical hazards. For tea, the chemical category explicitly includes pesticide and drug residues. The analysis must consider both probability and severity.

It also requires supplier evaluation and approval under § 1.505. Among other things, the importer should consider whether the supplier is subject to a warning letter, import alert or other FDA compliance action, and review the supplier's food safety history. Approval must be documented, and the supplier must be re-evaluated with new information and at least every 3 years.

Reason 4: Weak verification and recordkeeping

Even with a programme in place, shipments fail when verification is thin. Section 1.506 requires written procedures and documented decisions about which verification activities to use and how often. The options include onsite audits, sampling and testing, review of the supplier's food safety records, and other appropriate activities.

Two details are worth knowing. First, under § 1.506(e)(2)(ii), you generally may not rely on the foreign supplier itself, or its employees, to carry out verification activities — the exception is sampling and testing. Second, sampling and testing records must include the lot number, samples tested, tests and analytical methods, dates, results, corrective actions, lab identification, and confirmation that testing was done by a qualified individual. Financial conflicts of interest are prohibited, and payment must not be tied to results.

Records must be kept for at least 2 years under § 1.510, and each line entry must carry the importer's name, email address and FDA-recognised unique facility identifier (UFI) under § 1.509(a). If a supplier is found non-compliant, § 1.508 calls for prompt action, which may include discontinuing that supplier.

Small importers are not entirely exempt. Under § 1.512, a very small importer — one averaging less than $1 million per year in human food over the prior three years — has reduced requirements, but still must meet §§ 1.502, 1.503 and 1.509.

Reason 5: The firm is on an import alert

FDA Import Alerts are enforcement tools designed to keep violative products out of the country. An alert lists firms or products subject to detention without physical examination (DWPE). In practice, this means the product may be detained at the border without being physically examined, and the importer must provide evidence to overcome the appearance of a violation.

Two alerts are relevant context here. Import Alert 99-41 concerns DWPE based on FSVP violations. Import Alert 99-48 covers detention of foods due to chemical contamination, including substances such as benzene, dioxins, PCBs and PFAS. Note that these are general food and compliance alerts — there is no tea-specific FDA import alert to rely on or fear by name. Tea falls under the general chemical and food-safety framework.

To be removed from an import alert, the firm must provide evidence to the FDA showing it has resolved the conditions that led to the violation, as described in the FDA Regulatory Procedures Manual, Chapter 9-8. In common shorthand, the "red list" is firms currently subject to DWPE, and the "green list" is firms that have met the conditions for removal.

Bottom line

Tea usually fails a US customs check for reasons that have little to do with the leaf itself. File Prior Notice electronically and on time, respect the 2/4/8-hour clock and the earliest-filing windows, register the facility, run a real FSVP with a written hazard analysis and documented verification, keep records for two years, and check whether your supplier appears on an import alert. The paperwork is the gate; get it right and the tea gets through.
