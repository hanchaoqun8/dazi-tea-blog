# How Retailers Hide Poor Tea Test Results From Consumers

Most tea sellers are straightforward. But in any market, a few rely on the fact that buyers rarely ask to see the paperwork behind a "tested" claim. The result is a set of familiar tells — vague language, missing documents, and reassuring words that do not actually say much.

This article is not about naming names. It is about teaching you to recognize the patterns that can conceal weak test results, and to ask questions that get real answers. The same skills help consumers buying a tin of tea and small retailers vetting a supplier.

## First, understand what a real test report shows

You cannot spot a weak claim if you do not know what a strong one looks like. A proper Certificate of Analysis (COA) or test report should identify several things: the laboratory, including its name and accreditation; the sample or lot ID; the date the sample was taken and the date the report was issued; the analytes tested; the method used; the result with its unit; the LOQ and LOD; and a pass or fail against a stated limit.

That is a lot of detail, and each piece matters. A report that omits most of it is not necessarily fake, but it is not very useful either. When a seller says a tea is "lab tested" and produces nothing resembling this list, you are being asked to take a claim on trust.

One concept that helps throughout: "not detected" does not mean zero. It means the substance was below the detection capability of the method used. A result depends on what was tested, the method's limits, the sampling, and the timing. A seller who presents "not detected" as proof of absolute purity is oversimplifying — sometimes deliberately.

## Red flag 1: no COA available on request

The first and simplest tell is the absence of a COA. Ask for the test report for the lot you are buying. A supplier with nothing to hide will usually have a report they can share, or can request one from the producer.

A seller who deflects — "our tea is tested, trust us," "the report is confidential," "we cannot share it" — is giving you a answer that cannot be checked. There can be legitimate reasons a specific document is not immediately at hand, but a responsible supplier will explain what they can provide and why.

For a small retailer, this is a supplier-screening issue. Under the US FSVP framework, a supplier evaluation considers food safety history, including testing results and audits, and verification activities can include reviewing supplier records. A supplier who refuses to share test documentation makes that evaluation much harder. See [FSVP for tea importers explained](/posts/fsvp-tea-importers-explained/) for the structure behind this.

## Red flag 2: no lot number

A COA without a lot number is only loosely connected to the goods in front of you. The lot number is the thread that ties a test report to a specific batch, and therefore to the cartons you are buying.

Be cautious of tea whose packaging shows no batch or lot identifier, or whose lot number does not match the number on the report. If a seller offers a "tested" report for one lot while selling you tea from a different or unidentified lot, the report is not evidence about your purchase.

This matters because sampling is statistical. A test describes a sample from a particular lot, not every bag in a warehouse. Without a lot match, even a genuine clean report is the wrong document for your goods. Our article on [tea batch testing limits](/posts/tea-batch-testing-limits/) explains why one good test is not a blanket guarantee.

## Red flag 3: vague "tested" language

Words like "tested," "quality checked," "pure," or "pesticide-free" do very little work on their own. They do not say what was tested, by whom, using which method, against which limit, or when.

Useful language is specific. "Screened for [named analytes] by [lab] to [standard], result below [stated limit] for lot [number]" is a claim you can evaluate. "Tested in independent labs" is a claim you cannot.

Be especially wary of marketing phrases used as if they were regulatory categories. "Zero pesticide residue" is one such phrase — it is a marketing term, not a legal classification. We explain the distinction in [what zero pesticide residue really means](/posts/zero-pesticide-residue-meaning/). Similarly, organic certification is a process standard that restricts which substances may be used in production; it is not a guarantee of zero residues, and residue testing still matters. Our comparison of [organic versus conventional tea residue](/posts/organic-vs-conventional-tea-residue/) covers that point.

## Red flag 4: cherry-picked batches

Here is a subtler practice. A seller may hold a clean COA for one particularly good batch and use it to support claims about a much wider range of products. The report is genuine — it just is not about the tea being sold.

Watch for a single report that appears across many different products, grades, or harvest dates. Watch for a report whose date or lot number does not line up with the product description. Watch for a seller who cannot produce a report for the specific lot they are shipping today.

The defense is the same as above: match the report to the lot, the product, and the date. Ask directly, "Is this the report for the exact lot in this shipment?" The answer tells you a great deal.

## Red flag 5: the wrong scope or the wrong limit

A report can be technically genuine and still be beside the point. Common problems include:

- **The wrong analytes.** A report covering one group of pesticides says nothing about substances not included. Ask what was screened.
- **A high detection limit.** If the lab's LOQ is above the limit that matters in your market, a "not detected" result tells you very little.
- **No stated limit.** A result with no pass/fail against a named standard is hard to interpret.
- **The wrong market's limit.** Residue limits differ by jurisdiction. A result judged against one market's MRL is not automatically meaningful in another. The EU applies a 0.01 mg/kg default MRL where no specific MRL exists, as set out in Regulation 396/2005 — but other markets set their own rules. See [the EU 0.01 mg/kg default limit](/posts/eu-default-limit-0-01-mg-kg/) and [EU MRLs for tea](/posts/what-are-eu-mrls-for-tea/).

Understanding these details is easier once you know how to read the document itself. Our guide to [reading a tea lab test report](/posts/read-tea-lab-test-report/) is a good companion.

## Questions that cut through the vagueness

Instead of arguing about whether a seller is honest, ask questions that require specific answers.

- "Can you send the COA for this exact lot number?"
- "Which lab produced it, and are they accredited to ISO/IEC 17025?"
- "What analytes were tested, and what were the LOD and LOQ?"
- "What limit was each result compared against, and for which market?"
- "When was the sample taken, and when was the report issued?"
- "Does the lot number on the report match the cartons you are shipping?"

ISO/IEC 17025 is the international standard for the competence of testing and calibration laboratories, so it is a reasonable benchmark to ask about. A supplier who answers these questions clearly is showing you how their claim is supported. A supplier who cannot answer them is showing you something too. Our overview of [third-party tea testing labs](/posts/third-party-tea-test-labs/) explains what accreditation does and does not tell you.

## What this is not

This is not a recommendation to distrust every seller or to demand impossible proof. Testing has real limits — sampling is statistical, methods have detection limits, and no report can describe every leaf in a lot. The goal is not absolute certainty; it is to make sure the claims you rely on are specific, relevant, and documented.

A seller who says "here is the report for this lot, tested by an accredited lab for these substances against this limit" is giving you something usable. A seller who says only "trust us, it's tested" is not. The difference is not about personality; it is about whether the claim can be checked.

## Bottom line

Weak tea test results are often hidden behind vague "tested" claims, missing COAs, absent lot numbers, cherry-picked reports, and scopes that do not match the product or market. You do not need to accuse anyone — just ask for the COA for the exact lot, confirm lab accreditation, check analytes, LOD/LOQ, and the stated limit, and make sure the report matches the goods. Specific, documented answers are the difference between a claim you can rely on and one you cannot.
