# Pesticide Default Limit 0.01mg/kg In EU Tea Rules

If you have ever read an EU pesticide report on tea, you have probably met the number 0.01 mg/kg. It appears again and again, and it is widely misunderstood. Some people treat it as proof that EU rules demand absolutely zero pesticide residue. Others assume it is a scientific safety threshold — a line between "safe" and "unsafe."

Both readings are wrong. The 0.01 mg/kg figure is a legal default maximum residue level (MRL). Understanding exactly what that means will make you far better at reading test reports and supplier claims.

## Where the number comes from

The rule sits in Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 on maximum residue levels of pesticides in or on food and feed of plant and animal origin. Article 18(1) says that products listed in Annex I must not contain pesticide residues exceeding certain levels.

Those levels come in two forms. Article 18(1)(a) covers the specific MRLs set out in Annexes II and III. Article 18(1)(b) is the default rule that applies when no specific MRL exists.

The exact wording of the relevant part is:

> "0,01 mg/kg for those products for which no specific MRL is set out in Annexes II or III, or for active substances not listed in Annex IV..."

So the default applies in two situations: where a product has no specific MRL in Annexes II or III, and where an active substance is not listed in Annex IV. The regulation also allows different default values to be fixed under Article 45(2), and such default values are listed in Annex V.

That is the precise legal basis. Notice what it does not say: it does not say "zero," and it does not describe a toxicological safety threshold.

## The default is not "zero residue"

The most common mistake is to read 0.01 mg/kg as a zero-residue rule. It is not.

A default MRL is a compliance limit. It is the level above which a product is non-compliant with the regulation — and below which it is compliant, for the purpose of that specific rule. A tea can contain a measurable residue below 0.01 mg/kg and still be within the default limit.

This is closely tied to the idea of the limit of quantification, or LOQ. The 0.01 mg/kg default sits at the LOQ level. The LOQ is the lowest amount that can be quantified with acceptable precision and accuracy by an analytical method. In practice, the default MRL operates at the level where labs can reliably measure a residue.

This is also why "not detected" on a report should not be confused with "zero." A result reported as not detected means the substance was below the method's ability to detect it — it depends on what was tested, the method's detection capability, the sampling, and the timing. A product advertised as having "zero pesticide residue" is using a marketing phrase, not describing a regulatory category. We unpack that claim in [what zero pesticide residue really means](/posts/zero-pesticide-residue-meaning/).

## The default is not a safety threshold

The second mistake is to treat 0.01 mg/kg as a health-based safety line — as if anything below it is safe and anything above it is dangerous. The regulation does not present it that way.

An MRL is a legal limit for trade and compliance. It is set through a regulatory process that considers approved uses and residue behavior, and it is enforced as a rule for whether a product may be marketed. It is not a statement that a residue just under the number is harmless or that one just over it is harmful.

Keeping these ideas separate helps you read claims more carefully. A seller might say "this tea is below the EU limit," which is a compliance statement. That is very different from claiming the tea is "safe" in some absolute sense — and for a food product, broad health claims can raise entirely separate legal issues. Our article on [EU Regulation 396/2005 and tea](/posts/eu-regulation-396-2005-tea/) explains the wider framework.

## How this applies to tea specifically

Tea is a commodity under Annex I of Regulation 396/2005. It falls in category 06 — teas, coffee, and herbal infusions — under the code 0610000, described as "tea (dried leaves and stalks, fermented or otherwise, of *Camellia sinensis*)."

For many active substances, specific MRLs have been set on tea. Where a specific MRL exists, that specific value applies — you do not fall back on the 0.01 mg/kg default. The applicable value must be looked up per substance.

This is the part that trips up buyers. There is no single "tea MRL" number that covers every pesticide. Each active substance has its own entry, and the correct value depends on whether a specific MRL has been established for tea. Some substances may have a specific MRL higher than the default; others may fall under the default because no specific MRL exists. The only reliable approach is to check substance by substance.

The EU Pesticides Database is the official lookup tool. It is a web application that lets you search MRLs by product and substance. Because it is a JavaScript application, exact per-substance values are not available as simple plain text — which is why you should look values up in the database rather than trusting a number copied into a price list or a spreadsheet.

## How to look up a tea MRL in practice

If you need to verify a residue question, work through these steps.

- **Confirm the commodity code.** Tea is 0610000 in category 06 of Annex I. Make sure you are looking at the right commodity.
- **Identify the exact active substance.** "Pesticides" is not one thing. You need the specific compound name to search.
- **Look up the substance in the EU Pesticides Database.** Check whether a specific MRL exists for tea, and what the value is.
- **Check the current consolidated regulation.** Annexes II, III, IV, and V are amended frequently. An old copy of the regulation may not reflect the current limits.
- **Match the report to the limit.** When you receive a COA, compare the result for each analyte against the correct, current MRL for that substance on tea.

A useful habit is to keep a dated record of which version of the regulation and which database values you relied on. Because the annexes change, a value you checked months ago may no longer be current. The official consolidated text linked below is the version to check.

## A note on dilution and processing

One more rule is worth knowing. Chapter III of Regulation 396/2005 includes Article 19, which prohibits processing or diluting non-compliant products to bring them into compliance.

In plain terms, you cannot take a tea that exceeds an MRL and blend or process it down until the number looks acceptable. That is a legal prohibition, not a loophole. For buyers, it is a reason to treat an out-of-spec lot as a real problem rather than something to be fixed by mixing it with clean stock.

## Bottom line

The 0.01 mg/kg figure in EU tea rules is a legal default maximum residue level, not a zero-residue rule and not a safety threshold. It is set out in Article 18(1)(b) of Regulation 396/2005 and applies where no specific MRL exists in Annexes II or III, or where an active substance is not listed in Annex IV — operating at the LOQ level. Tea is a defined commodity (code 0610000), and many substances have their own specific MRLs, so always look up the exact value for each substance in the EU Pesticides Database and check the current consolidated regulation.
