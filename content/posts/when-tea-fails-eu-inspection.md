# What Happens When Tea Fails EU Pesticide Inspection

A test result comes back above the limit. The buyer calls, the shipment is on hold, and someone asks the question everyone asks at that moment: can we blend it down and still send it?

The short answer is no. European pesticide law is unusually clear on this point, and the rule catches people who assume that dilution is just good housekeeping. This article explains what the law actually says, why the result matters, and how to respond sensibly.

## Step one: confirm what "failed" means

Before reacting, make sure the failure is real and understood. A result means little without context. Check:

- **Which substance** was found, and what limit applies to it.
- **Which limit was used** — a specific MRL for that substance on tea, or the default.
- **Which version** of the regulation the comparison was based on, because the annexes change.
- **What the laboratory reported**, including the method, the unit, the LOQ and the sample or lot ID.

This is not pedantry. Regulation 396/2005 is amended frequently, and a comparison against an outdated annex is not a sound basis for a decision. Always check the current consolidated text.

## The two possible limits

Tea is a commodity under Annex I of Regulation 396/2005, in category 06 with code 0610000. Two kinds of limit can apply to a result.

**A specific MRL.** For many active substances, a specific maximum residue level exists for tea. That value must be looked up per substance in the EU Pesticides Database. There is no single table of tea numbers to rely on.

**The default MRL.** Where no specific MRL is set in Annexes II or III for that product, or where the active substance is not listed in Annex IV, Article 18(1) applies a default of **0.01 mg/kg**. The regulation's exact wording is "0,01 mg/kg for those products for which no specific MRL is set out in Annexes II or III, or for active substances not listed in Annex IV". Unless a different default is fixed under Article 45(2), these default values appear in Annex V.

This default is a legal compliance limit set at the level of quantification. It is not a "zero residue" rule and not a safety threshold. Knowing that prevents two mistakes: panicking over a detectable trace below the limit, and underestimating a result because it "sounds small".

## The rule that surprises people: no dilution

Here is the central point. Under Chapter III of the regulation — Articles 18 and 19 — **Article 19 prohibits processing or diluting non-compliant products in order to bring them into compliance.**

In practical terms, if your tea has residues above the applicable MRL or the 0.01 mg/kg default, you cannot:

- blend it with compliant tea to lower the average;
- process it in a way intended to reduce the residue so it passes;
- mix it into a larger lot to make the problem disappear.

The law does not allow a non-compliant product to be turned into a compliant one by these means. This matters because blending is completely normal in the tea trade for flavour and consistency. The purpose is what changes. Blending for taste is ordinary business; blending to dilute a known residue problem is something the regulation expressly forbids.

A useful way to remember it: compliance is a property of the lot you tested, not something you can manufacture afterwards by arithmetic.

## Why "not detected" is not the same as safe

Failures often follow a misunderstanding of laboratory language. If an earlier report said "ND" or "not detected", that did not mean zero. It meant the substance was below the method's detection capability.

**LOD** is the limit of detection — the lowest amount a method can detect. **LOQ** is the limit of quantification — the lowest amount it can measure with acceptable precision and accuracy. A lab with a high LOQ can produce a reassuring-looking ND while never having looked low enough to find a problem at the level that matters.

This is especially important with the 0.01 mg/kg default. Because that default sits at the level of quantification, the lab must be able to quantify at or below it for the result to be meaningful. If it cannot, the earlier "clean" report was weaker than everyone assumed, and a later failure is less of a mystery.

## Contaminants follow a parallel set of rules

Pesticide MRLs are not the only reason tea can fail an EU check. Regulation 1881/2006 sets maximum levels for certain contaminants in food, including metals and mycotoxins. Its key principle is that food above the annex limits may not be sold, and it too contains a **ban on mixing** compliant food with non-compliant food.

The same instinct applies across both regimes: you cannot hide a non-compliant lot inside a compliant one.

It is worth being precise about what the EU does and does not set here. EU law sets contaminant maximum levels in Regulation 1881/2006, but it does not set a single dedicated EU-wide lead limit specifically for tea leaves. Buyers therefore rely on import controls, national enforcement and their own COA testing. If you need a number for a specific contaminant, check the current official text rather than repeating a figure from memory.

For trace elements and other contaminants, Regulation 333/2007 sets sampling and analysis methods and rules on measurement uncertainty. Results may be reported as x +/- U with a coverage factor of 2 (about 95% confidence). A lot is accepted when the result does not exceed the maximum level after uncertainty and recovery are taken into account, and rejected when it exceeds beyond reasonable doubt. That framework governs contaminants; pesticide MRLs come from Regulation 396/2005.

## How to respond to a failed result

Once a result is confirmed, the sensible steps are practical rather than creative:

- **Isolate the lot.** Keep it separate from compliant stock so there is no accidental mixing.
- **Recheck the paperwork.** Confirm the substance, the limit, the annex version and the lab's LOQ.
- **Look at the sampling.** Consider whether the sample truly represented the lot, without using that as an excuse to dismiss a genuine result.
- **Do not dilute or reprocess** the tea to make it pass.
- **Talk to the buyer openly.** A documented, transparent problem is easier to manage than one that surfaces later.
- **Review the supply chain.** Find out where the substance came from before the next shipment.

There is no credible basis for assuming that time, storage or aging will reliably bring residues down to a compliant level. Degradation depends on the substance, the conditions and the time involved, and you should not assume a contaminated lot will become acceptable on its own.

## Bottom line

When tea fails an EU pesticide inspection, the law removes the easy way out. Article 19 of Regulation 396/2005 prohibits processing or diluting a non-compliant product into compliance, and Regulation 1881/2006 bans mixing compliant and non-compliant food for contaminants. Confirm the substance, the applicable MRL or 0.01 mg/kg default, the current annex version and the lab's LOQ — then isolate the lot and deal with it openly. There is no quick fix that the rules will accept.
