# What Is The Difference Between China GB Standard And EU MRL

If you buy Chinese tea or ship it into Europe, you will eventually meet two abbreviations: GB and MRL. They are not competing products with a single winner. They are two different regulatory systems that approach the same question — how much pesticide residue is allowed — in different ways.

The most useful thing you can learn is not a list of numbers. It is how each system is structured, and why a blanket statement like "China is looser" or "the EU is stricter" is too crude to be reliable. This article explains the framework in plain language and shows where to check the real values.

## Two systems, one basic idea

Both systems set maximum residue levels, usually shortened to MRLs. An MRL is the maximum amount of a pesticide residue that is permitted in or on a food. It is a legal compliance limit, not a statement that any amount below it is desirable and any amount above it is dangerous.

Both systems also cover tea. That shared starting point is where many of the differences become interesting.

## GB 2763: China's pesticide MRL standard

GB 2763 is China's national standard on maximum residue limits for pesticides in food, and it covers tea among many other foods. Think of it as the reference point a Chinese grower or exporter works to when asking whether a residue level is acceptable in the domestic market.

Two practical points about GB 2763:

- **It is updated periodically.** Revisions appear over time — the 2021 revision and later versions exist — so an old copy can be out of date. Always check the current official edition rather than relying on a number remembered from a previous year.
- **It sets limits per substance and per food.** Like other MRL systems, it does not provide one single number that applies to every pesticide on every crop.

Because version numbers and specific limits change, this article deliberately does not quote specific GB numeric limits for tea. If you need one, go to the current official text. A number copied into a sales listing or an old forum post is not a dependable source.

## EU MRLs: Regulation (EC) No 396/2005

On the European side, the governing text is Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 on maximum residue levels of pesticides in or on food and feed of plant and animal origin.

Tea appears in Annex I of that regulation as "tea (dried leaves and stalks, fermented or otherwise, of Camellia sinensis)," under category 06 — teas, coffee and herbal infusions — with code 0610000. Specific MRLs are set for many active substances on tea, and the applicable value must be looked up for each substance.

The regulation is amended frequently. Its annexes are updated as MRLs are added, changed or removed, so the current consolidated version is the one that counts. Article 18(1) is the core provision: products in Annex I must not contain residues above the MRLs set in Annexes II and III, or above the default where no specific MRL applies.

Chapter III, covering Articles 18 and 19, also prohibits processing or diluting non-compliant products to bring them into compliance. That matters for anyone tempted to treat a later step as a fix for a residue problem.

## The EU 0.01 mg/kg default — and what it is not

Here is the feature of the EU system that most often causes confusion.

Article 18(1) of Regulation 396/2005 sets a default of **0.01 mg/kg** for products where no specific MRL is set out in Annexes II or III, or for active substances not listed in Annex IV, unless different default values are fixed under Article 45(2). Those default values are listed in Annex V.

The regulation's exact wording refers to "0,01 mg/kg for those products for which no specific MRL is set out in Annexes II or III, or for active substances not listed in Annex IV."

Three things that the default is **not**:

- It is not a blanket "zero residue" rule. It is a legal limit, set at the level of quantification.
- It is not a safety threshold. It is a compliance line for enforcement.
- It is not a single number applied to every situation. Where a specific MRL exists, the specific MRL applies; the default fills the gaps.

That gap-filling role is what makes the EU system distinctive. If a substance has no specific MRL on tea, the default still gives an enforceable number. Other systems may handle that situation differently, and the outcome can differ as a result.

## Where the differences actually come from

Now we can answer the question honestly. Differences between GB 2763 and EU MRLs arise from several structural facts, not from one system being uniformly "strict."

**Different substances are covered differently.** A substance may have a specific MRL in one system and no specific MRL in the other. When the EU has no specific MRL, the 0.01 mg/kg default applies. That default is often stricter than a specific GB MRL set for the same substance. This is the most common reason people perceive the EU as stricter.

**Values differ by substance where both systems set one.** Two systems can set different numbers for the same pesticide on the same crop. Neither difference is a universal rule; each case stands on its own.

**Lists and annexes change at different times.** Both systems are revised periodically on their own schedules. A substance may be covered in one system at a moment when the other has not yet addressed it, or has changed a value.

**The commodity definition matters.** In the EU, tea is defined at the dried-leaf commodity level under code 0610000. How a system defines the food to which the limit applies affects how a result is judged.

So the accurate framing is a general tendency, not an absolute: because the EU applies the 0.01 mg/kg default where no specific MRL exists, EU limits can be stricter than GB for substances where GB sets a specific MRL. But you cannot conclude that GB is always looser, or that EU is always stricter, for every substance. Limits differ by substance, and the only reliable approach is to look each one up.

## Why "China is looser" is a bad shortcut

That shortcut causes real problems. An exporter may assume that meeting GB 2763 automatically means meeting EU rules. It does not. A tea can be acceptable in one market and non-compliant in the other, for a specific substance and a specific shipment.

The reverse mistake happens too. A buyer may assume that a tea sold in Europe must be "cleaner" in every respect. Compliance is measured against the applicable limits for the destination market, and those limits are substance-specific. There is no single purity ladder that every tea climbs.

For a small importer, the safe habit is simple: check the destination market's rules for the specific substance, using the official source, and do not let a general impression stand in for a lookup.

## How to check properly

A short routine keeps you on solid ground.

1. **Identify the substance.** "Pesticides" is too broad. You need the active substance name.
2. **Identify the commodity.** For the EU, tea is code 0610000 under Annex I of Regulation 396/2005.
3. **Check the current consolidated text.** Annexes II, III, IV and V are amended over time.
4. **Use the official lookup tool.** The EU Pesticides Database is the official MRL search. It is a JavaScript application, so exact per-substance values are not easily reproduced as plain text — use the database directly rather than trusting a copied figure.
5. **For GB 2763, consult the current official edition.** Do not rely on a number quoted in an article, including this one.
6. **Match the result to the lot.** A laboratory result is only meaningful when tied to the goods and read with its method and limit of quantification, consistent with the sampling principles reflected in Commission Regulation (EC) No 333/2007.

## Bottom line

GB 2763 is China's pesticide MRL standard covering tea, and EU MRLs are set through Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, where tea is commodity code 0610000. The structural difference that matters most is the EU 0.01 mg/kg default, which applies where no specific MRL exists and which is often stricter than a specific GB MRL for the same substance. That is a general tendency, not an absolute rule — limits differ by substance, and both systems change over time. Do not quote GB numbers from memory or assume one market is always stricter. Look up each substance in the current official sources, and tie any test result to the lot it describes.
