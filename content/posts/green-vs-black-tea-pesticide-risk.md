# Green Tea vs Black Tea: Pesticide Test Risk Comparison

Ask a room of tea drinkers which type carries more pesticide residue and you will get a confident answer — and several different ones. Some say green tea, because the leaves are less processed. Others say black tea, because it is the most traded. A few say it depends on the country of origin.

Here is the short version: there is no reliable basis for saying that green tea or black tea is inherently riskier. Residue risk is driven mainly by how the tea was grown and whether it was tested, not by which of these two styles it happens to be. The legal limits also apply to the same commodity in both cases. This article explains why, and what to look at instead.

## First, how the law sees both teas

Under Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, the tea commodity is defined as "tea (dried leaves and stalks, fermented or otherwise, of Camellia sinensis)." It sits in Annex I, category 06, under code 0610000.

Notice what that definition does. It covers tea whether it is fermented or not. In trade terms, black tea is fully oxidized and green tea is not, but from the perspective of this regulation both are the same commodity: dried leaves and stalks of the same plant. The applicable maximum residue levels are set for that commodity, per active substance.

That has an immediate consequence. There is not one set of legal limits for green tea and a different set for black tea. If you are checking compliance, you look up the substance on the dried-leaf commodity, and you check it the same way for either style. The EU Pesticides Database is the official tool for looking up current values. Because it is a JavaScript application, exact per-substance values are not readily reproduced as plain text — so use the database itself rather than a number copied into a product description.

The default rule matters too. Article 18(1) of the regulation sets a default of 0.01 mg/kg where no specific MRL is set out in the annexes, or for active substances not listed in Annex IV. That 0.01 mg/kg figure is a legal compliance limit at the quantification level. It is not a "zero residue" rule and not a safety threshold. It applies across the commodity, regardless of style.

## Processing differences: what they do and do not prove

Green and black tea take different paths after harvest. Green tea is typically fixed early to halt oxidation; black tea is withered, rolled and allowed to oxidise fully before drying. Those steps change the leaf's chemistry, colour and flavour.

It is tempting to leap from "the processing differs" to "therefore the residues differ in a predictable way." That leap is not supported. Several cautions apply:

- **Processing changes the leaf, not the farming history.** Whether a pesticide was used, which one, and when, is decided in the field. The factory steps come later.
- **Drying is common to both.** Both styles end as dried leaf. Drying concentrates what is in the leaf by removing water; it does not selectively remove a residue because of the style.
- **A breakdown story needs evidence.** If someone claims one process reduces a given residue, that is a claim about a specific substance under specific conditions. It is not a general property of green or black tea.

So processing is a real difference, but it is not a reliable predictor of residue risk on its own. The thing that determines whether a residue is present at all is what happened before processing.

## The leaf-to-cup question

Buyers often ask a slightly different question: "Even if the leaf has a residue, does any of it reach my cup?" This is the leaf-to-cup issue, and it deserves a careful answer.

The legal MRL applies to the dried-leaf commodity as placed on the market — the "tea (dried leaves and stalks...)" described in Annex I. It is not a limit set for a prepared beverage. What happens during brewing involves many variables: how much leaf is used, how long it steeps, water temperature, and the properties of the substance in question.

Two points follow.

First, you cannot conclude that a compliant leaf guarantees a particular result in the cup, or that a non-compliant leaf is automatically harmless once brewed. The regulation governs the commodity; brewing behaviour is a separate question and not something to estimate from a general rule.

Second, and more importantly for a buyer: you do not need to resolve the leaf-to-cup puzzle to make a sound purchase decision. If your concern is compliance, the right question is whether the dried leaf meets the applicable limit, and the right evidence is a test report on that leaf. Speculating about transfer rates is a distraction when a COA can answer the question directly.

This is also why "I rinse the leaves first" is not a compliance strategy. Rinsing may change what is in the cup, but it does not change the status of the commodity you bought or sold.

## What actually drives residue risk

If tea type is a weak predictor, what is a strong one? In practice, the useful questions are these.

**How was it grown?** Farming practices determine which substances are used, whether they are used close to harvest, and whether records are kept. A supplier who can speak clearly about crop management is more useful than one who only talks about flavour.

**Was it tested?** A Certificate of Analysis is evidence; a story is not. Testing turns a claim into a measurement.

**Was the right thing sampled?** Commission Regulation (EC) No 333/2007 covers sampling and analysis methods for contaminants and illustrates why the sample matters. One test of one bag does not prove a whole lot is compliant. Ask whether the report is tied to the lot you are buying.

**Are records current?** Limits and annexes are amended over time. A report from years ago may not reflect the current consolidated regulation, and the relevant MRL for a substance can change. Check the current official text and database.

None of these questions mention green or black. That is the point.

## Where the myths come from

A few claims circulate so widely that they are worth naming directly.

"A gentle process means fewer residues." Not as a general rule. Green tea is less oxidised than black tea, but oxidation is not a residue-removal step you can rely on.

"Black tea is washed, so it is cleaner." Some processing steps involve water, but "washed" is not a regulatory category and does not establish compliance. Only a measured result against the applicable limit does.

"Organic means residue-free." Organic certification restricts which substances may be used in production, but it is a process standard, not a guarantee. Drift or contamination can still occur, so residue testing still matters.

"Zero residue is a thing you can buy." Zero pesticide residue is a marketing phrase, not a regulatory category. "Not detected" on a report means the result was below the method's detection capability — not that no substance was ever used. What counts is what was tested, the method's limit of quantification, the sampling and the timing.

There are no reliable public figures to quote for "green tea fails more often than black tea" or the reverse. If you see a percentage attached to that claim, treat it as unsupported unless the source and method are shown.

## A practical comparison checklist

When choosing between a green and a black tea, use the same checklist for both:

1. Ask for a COA tied to the exact lot, listing the laboratory and its accreditation.
2. Check which analytes were tested and the limit of quantification for each.
3. Look at the sampling and reporting dates, and confirm the lot matches the goods.
4. Verify the applicable MRL in the EU Pesticides Database rather than trusting a listing.
5. Ask about growing and harvest practices — the part that actually shapes residue risk.
6. Keep the documents. If you resell, your file is your evidence.

Green and black tea may differ in flavour, colour and how they brew. For residue compliance, treat them as the same commodity and apply the same scrutiny to both.

## Bottom line

Green tea and black tea are the same commodity under Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 — "tea (dried leaves and stalks, fermented or otherwise)" — so the same MRL framework and the 0.01 mg/kg default apply to both. Processing differences do not make one style inherently safer, and brewing behaviour cannot be reduced to a general rule. Residue risk is driven mainly by farming practices, testing and documentation, not by tea type. Buy on the strength of a lot-matched COA, check values in the official EU Pesticides Database, and ignore claims that one style is automatically cleaner.
