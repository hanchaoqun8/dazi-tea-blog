# Common Pesticides That Cause Tea Shipments To Be Rejected In EU

Buyers often ask for a list: which pesticides most often get tea turned away at the EU border? It is a reasonable question, but the honest answer is less satisfying than the question suggests.

There is no reliable public figure ranking the pesticides that cause tea rejections, and this article will not invent one. Anyone who quotes a precise "top culprit" list without a traceable official source is guessing. What is useful — and what this guide explains — is the **mechanism** by which a shipment becomes non-compliant. Understand that, and you can protect your own goods without needing a league table.

## Why a "most common pesticides" list is hard to give

Two problems stand in the way of an honest list.

First, the rules are substance-by-substance and product-by-product. A pesticide that is fine on one crop can be a problem on tea, and a limit that applied last year may have been revised. Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 is amended frequently, so any fixed list ages quickly.

Second, individual MRL values for tea cannot be reliably quoted as plain text. The EU publishes them through the **EU Pesticides Database**, which is an interactive application. Unless a value is separately verified against the current official text, repeating a number from memory or an old spreadsheet is risky.

So instead of naming names, let us look at the four ways a residue becomes a rejection.

## The four mechanisms that actually cause trouble

### 1. A residue above a specific MRL

Under Article 18(1) of Regulation 396/2005, products in Annex I must not contain pesticide residues above the MRLs set in Annexes II and III. Tea is a commodity in Annex I under category 06, with the code **0610000**, described as "tea (dried leaves and stalks, fermented or otherwise, of *Camellia sinensis*)".

Specific MRLs exist for many active substances on tea. Where one exists for a given substance, that is the number that applies. A finding above it is non-compliant, full stop. This is the most straightforward route to a rejected lot, and the one a careful buyer can check in advance.

### 2. A residue above the 0.01 mg/kg default

Article 18(1) also sets a general default of **0.01 mg/kg**. The regulation's exact wording applies it to products "for which no specific MRL is set out in Annexes II or III, or for active substances not listed in Annex IV" (unless a different default is fixed under Article 45(2); such values appear in Annex V).

This default is the mechanism that catches people off guard. If no specific MRL has been set for a substance on tea, there is no free pass — the 0.01 mg/kg ceiling applies. A trader who assumes "no listed MRL means no limit" has misunderstood the law. In practice, this is one of the most important reasons a shipment can fail.

Remember what the figure is: a legal compliance limit set at the level of quantification. It is **not** a blanket zero-residue rule and **not** a safety threshold. But it is very much enforceable.

### 3. The LOQ effect: tiny findings count

Because the default sits at the quantification level, modern analytical methods can detect residues at or near the point where the law cares. A finding that would have been invisible with older, less sensitive equipment can now be measured and compared against a limit.

That is not a flaw in the system — it is why the law uses a quantification-level default. But it changes what a buyer should expect. "We tested and found only trace amounts" is not a defence if the amount is above the applicable MRL or default. The relevant question is always the number against the limit, not how small the number sounds.

### 4. Sampling and uneven distribution

Testing is statistical. A lab analyses a small sample drawn from a much larger lot. If residues are concentrated on some leaves rather than spread evenly, the sample can either miss the problem or, in a different draw, find it. This variability is one reason results can differ between laboratories and why a single "clean" report is not a guarantee for the whole lot.

Good sampling practice — taking material from multiple points in the lot and documenting how it was done — is part of compliance, not an optional extra. A shipment rejected on resampling can be just as costly as one rejected on the first test, and more confusing.

## Which substance groups draw scrutiny

No honest article can say "these three pesticides cause most tea rejections" without data to back it. What can be said is which **categories of substances** residue programmes and buyers commonly examine, and why.

- **Insecticides** — a broad group used against pests during cultivation; many are included in routine multi-residue screens.
- **Fungicides** — used to control fungal diseases in the field; also commonly covered by screening panels.
- **Herbicides** — used for weed control; another standard category in residue monitoring.

These are generic classes, not a claim about which specific molecule fails most often. The practical point is that a serious tea screen covers many substances across these groups, because a problem can come from any of them. A panel that only looks at one group leaves gaps.

To move from categories to actual substances, use the official database (steps below). That is where you find out whether a specific active substance has a specific MRL on tea or falls back to the 0.01 mg/kg default.

## The rule that removes the easy fix

A question that surfaces after a failed test is whether the lot can be blended with clean tea to dilute the residue below the limit. **Article 19 of Regulation 396/2005 prohibits processing or diluting non-compliant products to bring them into compliance.**

This matters commercially. If a supplier responds to a residue finding with an offer to "mix it down," that is not a compliance solution. The proper response is to find the source, correct the production or supply problem, and verify the result with appropriate testing. Buyers should treat a dilution proposal as a serious warning sign about the supplier's understanding of the rules.

## How to check the substances that matter to your tea

Here is a practical way to work with the official source rather than guesswork.

1. Open the EU Pesticides Database MRL search (linked under Sources).
2. Select the commodity **tea** (code 0610000), or enter it in the product search.
3. Search for each active substance of interest.
4. Record the applicable MRL and any footnotes, along with the date you checked.
5. If no specific MRL appears, work to the 0.01 mg/kg default.

Keep a dated record of what you looked up. Because the database is interactive and the annexes change, your snapshot is useful evidence of what the position was when you made the decision.

## A note on comparing standards

Buyers sometimes assume Chinese limits are simply "looser" than EU ones. The reality is more nuanced. China's GB 2763 standard sets pesticide MRLs across foods including tea, and it is updated periodically. Limits differ substance by substance.

A general tendency is worth understanding: where a substance has a specific GB MRL but no specific EU MRL on tea, the EU's 0.01 mg/kg default will often be the stricter figure. That is a tendency, not an absolute rule, and it is another reason to check each substance rather than comparing standards as a whole.

## What a buyer should do

- Ask suppliers which pesticides were included in the test panel, not just whether the tea "passed."
- Confirm the limit used for each result — specific MRL or default.
- Check key substances yourself in the EU database and keep dated records.
- Insist on good sampling documentation for the lot.
- Reject dilution as a fix for a non-compliant lot.

## Bottom line

There is no trustworthy public list of the "most common" pesticides rejected in EU tea, and inventing one would be misleading. Rejections happen through a small set of mechanisms: a residue above a specific MRL, a residue above the 0.01 mg/kg default where no specific MRL exists, findings at the quantification level, and sampling variability. Insecticides, fungicides and herbicides are the broad groups typically screened, but you must check individual substances in the EU Pesticides Database. And no failing lot may be brought into compliance by dilution — Article 19 forbids it.
