# FSVP For Tea Importers: FDA Supplier Verification Explained

If you import tea into the United States, you cannot outsource your FSVP to the seller. The Foreign Supplier Verification Program is the importer's own obligation, and the regulations say so plainly. A supplier's certificate may be useful evidence, but it is not your program.

FSVP lives in **21 CFR Part 1, Subpart L**, with its statutory basis in section 805 of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. 384a). This guide walks through what a compliant file looks like for a tea importer, using the section numbers so you can check each point against the official text.

## Who counts as the importer

Start with § 1.500, because it decides whose duty this is. The "importer" is generally the **US owner or consignee at the time of entry**. If there is no US owner or consignee, it is the US agent or representative of the foreign owner or consignee, acting with signed consent.

This is important for small buyers. If you are the US owner or consignee of the tea, the FSVP duty is yours — even if the grower, packer or exporter overseas arranged most of the production.

## The core promise you must make

Under § 1.502(a), for each food you import you must develop, maintain and follow an FSVP that gives **adequate assurance** that the foreign supplier produces the food using processes providing at least the same level of public health protection as applicable US requirements (including FSMA sections 418 or 419 where relevant), and that the food complies with §§ 402 and 403(w) of the Act.

Two habits follow from this wording:

- The program is **specific to each food** and its supplier, not a generic company policy.
- It must be **developed and performed by a "qualified individual"** (§ 1.503) — someone with the education, training or experience to do the work, including the ability to read the records involved. Onsite audits, where used, must be performed by a **qualified auditor**.

If nobody on your team is a qualified individual, that is the first gap to close, either by training or by engaging someone who meets the definition.

## Step 1: Write the hazard analysis

Under § 1.504, you must prepare a **written hazard analysis**, regardless of whether you conclude there are no hazards requiring control. For each food, identify and evaluate known or reasonably foreseeable hazards, including:

- **Biological hazards** — for example parasites or pathogens.
- **Chemical hazards** — including **pesticide and drug residues**, natural toxins, unapproved food additives and allergens.
- **Physical hazards** — such as stones, glass or metal.

For tea, the chemical category deserves particular attention, since pesticide residues are explicitly named in the regulation. Your analysis must evaluate both the **probability** and the **severity** of each hazard. A hazard that is unlikely but serious still needs to be thought through, and a likely but minor one still belongs in the document.

The analysis is the foundation of everything that follows. Supplier approval and verification decisions are supposed to flow from it, not sit beside it.

## Step 2: Evaluate and approve the supplier

Under § 1.505, you evaluate the supplier using your hazard analysis, then approve it and document the decision. Relevant considerations include:

- which hazards are controlled by the supplier;
- the supplier's procedures and applicable FDA regulations;
- whether the supplier is subject to a **warning letter, import alert or other FDA compliance action**;
- the supplier's food safety history — testing results, audits, and how responsive it has been to problems.

Approval is not a one-off event. You must **reevaluate the supplier when new information arises and at least every three years**. If a supplier stops responding to questions or a test result changes, that is new information — do not wait for the three-year mark.

## Step 3: Choose and document verification activities

Under § 1.506, you must establish **written procedures** describing which verification activities you will use and how often. The regulation lists several options:

- **(A) onsite audits** of the supplier;
- **(B) sampling and testing**;
- **(C) review of the supplier's food safety records**;
- **(D) other appropriate activities**.

You decide and document which activities fit the hazards you identified. There is also a specific trigger: where a hazard is controlled by the supplier and there is a reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences or death, an **onsite audit is required before initial import and at least annually**, unless you can adequately justify a different approach.

### Who may perform verification

§ 1.506(e)(2)(ii) draws a clear line: you may **not** rely on the foreign supplier — or its employees — to perform verification activities, with one exception. **Sampling and testing** may be performed by the supplier. This is a common area of confusion, because a supplier-arranged lab test can be part of your evidence, but a supplier's own audit of itself cannot.

### What testing records must contain

If you rely on sampling and testing, § 1.506(e)(1)(ii) sets out what the records must include: the lot number, samples tested, the tests and **analytical methods** used, dates, results, corrective actions, identification of the laboratory, and a statement that the testing was performed by a qualified individual.

A loose summary on an email will not meet that standard. The record should let a reviewer reconstruct what was sampled, how it was tested and what came back.

### Keep conflicts of interest out

Under § 1.506(e)(4), there must be **no financial conflicts of interest**, and payment must not be tied to the results. In practice, that means you should not compensate a tester in a way that rewards a favourable finding. An independent laboratory and a clear fee arrangement are easier to defend.

## Step 4: Act when something is wrong

Under § 1.508, if you discover the supplier is not compliant, take **prompt and appropriate action**. The regulation expressly includes discontinuing the supplier as one possible response. "Appropriate" depends on the hazard and the circumstances, but "do nothing and keep importing" is not a defensible choice once you know there is a problem.

## At the border: entry identification

Under § 1.509(a), for each line entry you must provide electronically to CBP:

- the importer's **name**;
- the importer's **email address**;
- an FDA-recognized **unique facility identifier (UFI)**.

This is a data field at entry, not a document you hand to the border officer. Missing or inconsistent entry data is a separate practical headache from the underlying FSVP file, so check both.

## Keep the records

Under § 1.510, retain records for **at least two years after they are created or obtained**. For records about your processes and procedures, keep them for at least **two years after their use is discontinued**. If you ever need to show an auditor what you did, the records are the program — an unwritten program is very hard to demonstrate.

## Exemptions and the very small importer

Not every FSVP duty applies to everyone. The regulation includes several exemptions:

- **§ 1.501** — covers, among other things, juice and fish under HACCP, research or evaluation food, food for personal consumption, alcoholic beverages, transshipment, goods for processing and re-export, US-returned goods, and USDA-exclusive meat, poultry and egg products.
- **§ 1.511** — certain dietary supplements.
- **§ 1.513** — countries with recognized equivalent systems.
- **§ 1.512** — the **very small importer** exemption. This applies to a human food importer averaging **less than $1 million per year** over the previous three years. It reduces requirements, but it does not remove them: such importers still must comply with **§§ 1.502, 1.503 and 1.509**.

Do not assume the small-importer route means "no FSVP." It means a lighter version with a core still in place.

## What happens when FSVP fails

FDA uses Import Alerts as an enforcement tool. **Import Alert 99-41** provides for detention without physical examination based on FSVP violations. That means goods can be held at the border without a physical exam, and the importer must provide evidence to overcome the appearance of a violation.

If your file is thin — no written hazard analysis, no documented supplier approval, no verification records — remedying the situation after a detention is much harder than building the file before you ship.

## Bottom line

FSVP is the US importer's own responsibility. Identify who the importer is (§ 1.500), have a qualified individual develop the program (§§ 1.502–1.503), write a hazard analysis that names chemical hazards such as pesticide residues (§ 1.504), evaluate and approve the supplier and reevaluate at least every three years (§ 1.505), document your verification activities and keep them independent of the supplier except for testing (§ 1.506), act promptly on problems (§ 1.508), supply the name, email and UFI at entry (§ 1.509), and keep records for at least two years (§ 1.510). Very small importers get reduced duties, not none. Build the file before the goods move.
